Oxford BioData aims to use advanced AI to shape the future of enzyme design.

Use cases across therapeutics, industrial chemicals and consumer products have shown that enzymatic biocatalysis can access hard-to-reach chemistry and cut costs several-fold. Other areas look well suited to enzymes but remain far from their full commercial and technical potential: from rare-disease treatment through mining to terraformation.

Many of the hardest challenges those areas present require new-to-nature chemistry. That demands a more comprehensive and precision-driven computational toolkit for designing enzymes with specific functional properties than any platform currently offers.

Our team at Oxford BioData wants to remedy that. Our primary focus is bridging the physics-based fundamentals of enzyme catalysis and the achievements of modern ML. On one side, that involves quantum-mechanical and molecular dynamics simulations of atom-level interactions in the transition state. On the other, we aim to make those simulations computationally efficient by integrating them in a unified framework with protein language models (pLMs) and other state-of-the-art tools of bio-ML.

Our ambition is a comprehensive platform for de novo enzyme design. We want to empower researchers and firms across the economy to consider biosynthetic pathways that are either hard to identify, given the novel character of the elementary reactions, or inaccessible to directed evolution alone, often because characterised enzyme libraries are limited.

We are application-agnostic: we will pursue the most lucrative opportunities to deploy our design engine, and those will abound as more sophisticated manufacturing and technology demand novel chemical solutions.

An adjacent area we are exploring is reinforcement-learning (RL) environments for simulating scientific workflows. Besides complementing our work on homogenising wet-lab data for ML training, it holds vast commercial potential in its own right.

Our team comes from diverse backgrounds, with research experience at the University of Oxford, the University of Warwick, the Max Planck Institute of Molecular Cell Biology, Carnegie Mellon University and beyond. We are keen to generate innovative solutions and to see them have real-world impact.
